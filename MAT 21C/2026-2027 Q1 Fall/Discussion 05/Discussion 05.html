§I Warm-Up

(Warm-Up) Consider the dot product and my strange star product (component-wise multiplication):

⟨4,0⟩⋅⟨3,2⟩=(4)(3)+(0)(2)=43,⟨4,0⟩⋆⟨3,2⟩=⟨(4)(3),(0)(2)⟩=⟨43,0⟩.

If we rotate the vectors by 30°, we will get (I computed for you)

⟨4,0⟩↦⟨23,2⟩and⟨3,2⟩↦⟨12,332⟩.

Compute the dot product and my strange star product of the rotated vectors

⟨23,2⟩⋅⟨12,332⟩and⟨23,2⟩⋆⟨12,332⟩.
  • Are the new and old dot products equal?
  • Are the new and old star products related by a 30° rotation?

Solution. The dot product of the rotated vectors is

⟨23,2⟩⋅⟨12,332⟩=(23)(12)+(2)(332)=3+33=43,

which is the same as the dot product of the original vectors. The star product of the rotated vectors is

⟨23,2⟩⋆⟨12,332⟩=⟨(23)(12),(2)(332)⟩=⟨3,33⟩,

which is not the same as the star product of the original vectors and also not a 30° rotation of it. If we draw ⟨3,33⟩, it will be even higher than the ⟨3,2⟩ vector, so the angle between the two start products is greater than 30°. Even their lengths are different! We have

|⟨43,0⟩|=43≈6.9and|⟨3,33⟩|=3+27=30≈5.5.

The key takeaway is that the dot product is geometric: the choice of coordinates is irrelevant to dot product, we can find the dot product between two vectors by measuring their length and angle of separation.

In contrast, my strange star products are not geometric: if we are given two vectors without coordinates, we cannot compute the star product, and worse, if we will get different results if we choose two different coordinates to represent the vectors. You should never use my strange star product! (I see it on exams every time, and I hope I see it less!)

The reason why we love dot product and cross product is that (up to scaling) they are the only two distributive geometric operations involving vectors and scalars!

§II Dot Product

I have foreshadowed that we can compute the dot product of two vectors in the air by measuring their lengths and the angle of separation. Here is exactly how:

𝑢⃗⋅𝑣⃗=|𝑢⃗||𝑣⃗|cos𝜃.

In words, the dot product of two vectors is the product of their lengths and the cosine of the angle between them. Given the components of the vectors, we can also compute the dot product through

⟨𝑢1,𝑢2⟩⋅⟨𝑣1,𝑣2⟩=𝑢1𝑣1+𝑢2𝑣2,⟨𝑢1,𝑢2,𝑢3⟩⋅⟨𝑣1,𝑣2,𝑣3⟩=𝑢1𝑣1+𝑢2𝑣2+𝑢3𝑣3.

Dot product is especially interesting if one of the vectors is a unit vector (i.e. a vector of length 1, a vector that lies on the unit circle if placed at the origin) because then the dot product is the length of the projection of the other vector onto the unit vector. For example, given a vector 𝑎⃗=⟨𝑎1,𝑎2,𝑎3⟩, the (signed) length of the projection of 𝑎⃗ onto the unit vector 𝑖̂ pointing in the 𝑥-direction is simply 𝑎1.

This is codified in the dot product

𝑎⃗⋅𝑖̂=𝑎1=|𝑎⃗|cos𝜃.

In this case, we also easily see that the two ways of computing the dot product are the same because

cos𝜃=adjacenthypotenuse=𝑎1|𝑎⃗|⟹|𝑎⃗|cos𝜃=𝑎1.

It is worth nothing that when 𝑎1 is negative, then the angle 𝜃 is obtuse (so cos𝜃 is negative), which is why the dot product with a unit vector is the signed length of the projection (negative if the projection is in the opposite direction of the unit vector).

In general, the signed length of the projection of a vector 𝑎⃗ onto a vector 𝑏⃗ is given by

|𝑎⃗|cos𝜃=𝑎⃗⋅𝑏⃗|𝑏⃗|

and to find the projection vector, we simply do length times direction of the vector 𝑏⃗

proj𝑏⃗(𝑎⃗)=(𝑎⃗⋅𝑏⃗|𝑏⃗|)𝑏⃗|𝑏⃗|.

Note that direction is synonymous with unit vector, the direction of 𝑏⃗ means the unit vector 𝑏⃗/|𝑏⃗|.

(Exercise)

  1. Assume |𝑣⃗|=2 and |𝑢⃗|=3 and the angle between them is 𝜋3.

    • Find the dot product 𝑣⃗⋅𝑢⃗.
    • Find |𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗|. Hint: |𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗|=(𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗)⋅(𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗)
    • (Bonus) Give an example of two vectors 𝑣⃗=⟨…,…⟩ and 𝑢⃗=⟨…,…⟩ that satisfy the above conditions. Calculate 𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗ and its length.
  2. Find the projection of ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto ⟨2,1,−1⟩.
  3. Find the projection of ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto the plane described by 2𝑥+𝑦−𝑧=0 (the is the plane perpendicular to the vector ⟨2,1,−1⟩ that passes through the origin).

Solution.

  1. The dot product is

    𝑣⃗⋅𝑢⃗=|𝑣⃗||𝑢⃗|cos𝜃=(2)(3)cos(𝜋3)=6(12)=3.

    We have

    |𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗|2=(𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗)⋅(𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗)=𝑣⃗⋅𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗⋅𝑣⃗−2𝑣⃗⋅𝑢⃗+4𝑢⃗⋅𝑢⃗=|𝑣⃗|2−4(𝑢⃗⋅𝑣⃗)+4|𝑢⃗|2(the dot product is symmetric)=4−4(3)+4(9)=4−12+36=28.

    Therefore, |𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗|=28=27.

    Note that the dot product is distributive, so (𝑎⃗−𝑏⃗)⋅𝑐⃗=𝑎⃗⋅𝑐⃗−𝑏⃗⋅𝑐⃗ for example.

    An example of two vectors that satisfy the conditions is 𝑣⃗=⟨1,3⟩ and 𝑢⃗=⟨3,0⟩. We have

    𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗=⟨1,3⟩−2⟨3,0⟩=⟨−5,3⟩⟹|𝑣⃗−2𝑢⃗|=(−5)2+(3)2=25+3=28=27.

  2. We can find the length of projection of ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto ⟨2,1,−1⟩ by

    ⟨3,4,4⟩⋅⟨2,1,−1⟩6=3⋅2+4⋅1+4⋅(−1)6=66=6.

    The projection of ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto ⟨2,1,−1⟩ is the length of projection multiplied by the unit vector pointing in the direction of ⟨2,1,−1⟩, which is

    6⋅⟨2,1,−1⟩6=⟨2,1,−1⟩.

  3. By examining the following picture, we see that the sum of the projection of ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto a plane and the projection onto the normal vector of the plane should be ⟨3,4,4⟩. In technical terms, a vector can always be decomposed into a component parallel to a vector and a component orthogonal to the vector.

    Thus, the projection 𝑣⃗ of the vector ⟨3,4,4⟩ onto the plane is given by

    ⟨2,1,−1⟩+𝑣⃗=⟨3,4,4⟩.

    Solving for 𝑣⃗ either by just thinking about it or doing subtraction gives us

    𝑣⃗=⟨1,3,5⟩.

§III Cross Product

Cross product between two vectors 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗ calculates the area of the parallelogram described by 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗ and the direction perpendicular to the parallelogram according to the right hand rule.

For example in the above picture, we have

3𝑖̂×(𝑖̂+2𝑗̂)=6𝑘̂

because the parallelogram described by 3𝑖̂ and 𝑖̂+2𝑗̂ has area 6 (base is 3, height is 2) and the direction perpendicular to the parallelogram is in the 𝑧-direction, and we should take the positive 𝑘̂ direction according to the right hand rule.

In general, we can calculate the cross product of 𝑎⃗=⟨𝑎1,𝑎2,𝑎3⟩ and 𝑏⃗=⟨𝑏1,𝑏2,𝑏3⟩ by

𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗=|𝑖̂𝑗̂𝑘̂𝑎1𝑎2𝑎3𝑏1𝑏2𝑏3|.

For example, we can verify that

3𝑖̂×(𝑖̂+2𝑗̂)=|𝑖̂𝑗̂𝑘̂300120|=𝑖̂|0020|−𝑗̂|3010|+𝑘̂|3012|=6𝑘̂.
Remember that the way to calculate the determinant of a 2×2 matrix is |𝑎𝑏𝑐𝑑|=𝑎𝑑−𝑏𝑐, that is the product of the main diagonal minus the product of the other diagonal.

We can also use cross product to find areas of shapes associated with parallelograms. For example, the area of the triangle described by 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗ is half the area of the parallelogram described by 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗, so

Area of triangle described by𝑎⃗and𝑏⃗=12|𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|.

Before we move on to exercises, let us briefly mention the idea of shapes as equations. In 3-dimensional space, a surface corresponds to an equation and an equation corresponds to a surface. For example, the 𝑦𝑧-plane corresponds to the equation 𝑥=0 because the condition for a point (𝑥,𝑦,𝑧) to be on the 𝑥𝑦-plane is exactly that 𝑥=0. Say we want to know whether (1,0,3) is on the 𝑥𝑦-plane, we can simply check 1=?0, and the answer is no, so the point is not on the 𝑥𝑦-plane.

As another example, when we see the equation 𝑥2+𝑦2=1, we should immediately recognize that it is some surface in 3-dimensional space—it is the surface of a cylinder with radius 1 whose axis is the 𝑧-axis. If we want to know whether (1,0,3) is on the surface, we can check 12+02=?1, and the answer is yes, so the point is on the surface.

The next level of this idea is that the intersection of two surfaces correspond to a pair of equations. To check whether a point is on the intersection of two surfaces, we have to check whether it satisfies both equations. For example, (1,0,3) is not on the intersection of the surfaces 𝑥2+𝑦2=1 and 𝑥=0 because it does not satisfy the second equation.

To “find” the intersection of two surfaces amounts to simplifying a pair of equations to a pair of simpler equations. Keep in mind that a pair of equations usually cannot be simplified into a single equation (it is analogous to saying that the intersection of two surfaces is usually not a surface).

In the example above, we find that

{𝑥2+𝑦2=1𝑥=0⟺{𝑦2=1𝑥=0⟺{𝑦=1or𝑦=−1𝑥=0

which tells us that points on the intersection are of the form (0,1,anything) or (0,−1,anything). These draw out two lines which should make geometric sense by thinking about the cylinder along the 𝑧-axis and the 𝑦𝑧-plane.

It would be wrong to say that the pair of equations simplifies to a single equation 𝑦2=1 because this will describe some surface (in fact, it will be two surfaces) and we know the intersection of the cylinder and the plane is not a surface.

(Exercise)

  1. Find a nonzero vector orthogonal to ⟨1,−2,1⟩ and ⟨2,1,−1⟩.

  2. Two of the following points are in the the intersection of the planes described by 𝑥−2𝑦+𝑧=0 and 2𝑥+𝑦−𝑧=5. Find them.

    • (1,3,5)
    • (1,−2,−5)
    • (2,1,0)
    • (0,5,10)

    The intersection of the two planes is a line. Find a nonzero vector parallel to the line by taking the difference of the two points you found. Is this vector related to your answer to part 1?

  3. Consider the parallelepiped described by the vectors

    𝑎⃗=⟨3,0,0⟩,𝑏⃗=⟨1,2,0⟩,𝑐⃗=⟨1,1,2⟩.

    With the parallelogram described by 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗ as the base, find the height of the parallelepiped. Then, find the volume of the parallelepiped.

Solution.

  1. We can find a nonzero vector orthogonal to ⟨1,−2,1⟩ and ⟨2,1,−1⟩ by taking the cross product of the two vectors:

    ⟨1,−2,1⟩×⟨2,1,−1⟩=|𝑖̂𝑗̂𝑘̂1−2121−1|=𝑖̂|−211−1|−𝑗̂|112−1|+𝑘̂|1−221|=1𝑖̂+3𝑗̂+5𝑘̂.

  2. We can check if a point is in the intersection of the two planes by substituting the coordinates of the point into the equations of the planes and checking if both equations hold.

    • For (1,3,5), we have 1−2(3)+5=0 and 2(1)+3−5=0≠5, so it is not in the intersection.
    • For (1,−2,−5), we have 1−2(−2)+(−5)=0 and 2(1)+(−2)−(−5)=5, so it is in the intersection.
    • For (2,1,0), we have 2−2(1)+0=0 and 2(2)+1−0=5, so it is in the intersection.
    • For (0,5,10), we have 0−2(5)+10=0 and 2(0)+5−10=−5≠5, so it is not in the intersection.

    Therefore, the two points in the intersection are (1,−2,−5) and (2,1,0).

    A nonzero vector parallel to the line of intersection can be found by taking the difference of the two points:

    ⟨2,1,0⟩−⟨1,−2,−5⟩=⟨1,3,5⟩.

    This is exactly the same vector we found in part 1! This is no coincidence because vectors parallel to the planes 𝑥−2𝑦+𝑧=0 and 2𝑥+𝑦−𝑧=5 are orthogonal to ⟨1,−2,1⟩ and ⟨2,1,−1⟩ respectively, so a vector parallel to the line of intersection should be orthogonal to both ⟨1,−2,1⟩ and ⟨2,1,−1⟩, so part 1 and part 2 are secretly the same question.

  3. Exactly like the example of 3𝑖̂×(𝑖̂+2𝑗̂)=6𝑘̂, we have

    𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗=|𝑖̂𝑗̂𝑘̂300120|=𝑖̂|0020|−𝑗̂|3010|+𝑘̂|3012|=6𝑘̂.

    This tells us that the area of the parallelogram described by 𝑎⃗ and 𝑏⃗ is |6𝑘̂|=6. Looking at the parallelepiped in the figure, we see that the height of the parallelepiped is exactly the 𝑧-component of 𝑐⃗, which is 2.

    Therefore, the volume of the parallelepiped is 6×2=12.

    Note that we can have 𝑐⃗=⟨anything,anything,2⟩ and the height of the parallelepiped will still be 2. In general, the height of the parallelepiped is the length of the projection of 𝑐⃗ onto the direction that is perpendicular to the base, which is the direction of 𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗. In symbols, this means that the height of the parallelepiped described by 𝑎⃗, 𝑏⃗, and 𝑐⃗ is (the absolute value of)

    𝑐⃗⋅(𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|).

    The area of the base is |𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|, so the volume of the parallelepiped in general is (the absolute value of)

    𝑐⃗⋅(𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|)⏟height|𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗|⏟base=𝑐⃗⋅(𝑎⃗×𝑏⃗).

    Kind of neat, don’t you think?

    𝑢⃗⋅(𝑣⃗×𝑤⃗)=|𝑢1𝑢2𝑢3𝑣1𝑣2𝑣3𝑤1𝑤2𝑤3|?

    The geometric meaning of the determinant of a matrix is actually the volume of the parallelogram or the parallelepiped described by the rows (or columns) of the matrix!
