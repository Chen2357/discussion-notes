§I Taylor’s Formula

Taylor series are unreasonably effective at being equal to the original function! Consider a function 𝑓(𝑥) that looks just like the function 𝑥 on the interval [−1,1]. We have

𝑓(0)=0,𝑓′(0)=1,𝑓″(0)=0,𝑓(3)(0)=0,⋯,

so the Taylor series of 𝑓(𝑥) at 𝑥=0 is just

𝑓(0)+𝑓′(0)𝑥+𝑓″(0)𝑥22!+𝑓(3)(0)𝑥33!+⋯=𝑥.

However, there is nothing preventing me from defining 𝑓(𝑥) to be something completely different from 𝑥 outside of the interval [−1,1], then the Taylor series would be totally off despite being convergent for all 𝑥.

It is magical that for many functions we are interested in, the Taylor series whenever is convergent, converges to the original function. Taylor’s formula tells us the condition for when a function has this nice property.

(Taylor’s Formula) The remainder term

𝑅𝑛(𝑥)=𝑓(𝑥)−(𝑓(0)+𝑓′(0)𝑥+𝑓″(0)𝑥22!+⋯+𝑓(𝑛)(0)𝑥𝑛𝑛!)

after approximating 𝑓(𝑥) by the Taylor polynomial of degree 𝑛 is bounded by

|𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤𝑀|𝑥|𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!

where 𝑀 is an upper bound for |𝑓(𝑛+1)(𝑐)| for all 𝑐 between 0 and 𝑥.

If we can show that

lim𝑛→∞|𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤lim𝑛→∞𝑀|𝑥|𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!=0,

then we can conclude that the equality

𝑓(𝑥)=𝑓(0)+𝑓′(0)𝑥+𝑓″(0)𝑥22!+𝑓(3)(0)𝑥33!+⋯,

finally establishing the relationship between the function and its Taylor series.

As an example, the Taylor series of 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑒𝑥 is

1+𝑥+𝑥22!+𝑥33!+𝑥44!+⋯.

The exercise last week was using ratio test to show that this series converges for all 𝑥, but does it really converge to 𝑒𝑥?

Let us say we want to show that the series converges to 𝑒7 at 𝑥=7. We can write down the remainder

𝑅𝑛(7)=𝑒7−(1+7+722!+733!+⋯+7𝑛𝑛!)

which is bounded by

|𝑅𝑛(7)|≤𝑀7𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!.

We can choose 𝑀=𝑒7 since that is the maximum value of |𝑓(𝑛+1)(𝑐)| on the interval between 0 and 𝑥=7. Therefore, we have

|𝑅𝑛(7)|≤𝑒77𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!.

This inequality is of interest on its own. It tells us how good our approximation of 𝑒7 is using just the first 𝑛 terms of the Taylor series. For example, it tells us that the difference between 𝑒7 and

1+7+722!+733!+⋯+74040!

is at most

𝑒774141!≲1.462×10−12.

This tells us that the approximation is accurate to 12 decimal places!

To show that

1+7+722!+733!+⋯=𝑒7

amounts to showing that

lim𝑛→∞|𝑅𝑛(7)|≤lim𝑛→∞𝑒77𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!=0

which is true because lim𝑛→∞𝑥𝑛/𝑛!=0 for all 𝑥 (which is on our formula sheet).

(Exercise)

  1. Show that

    1+9+922!+933!+⋯=𝑒9.

    (The solution is simply replacing 7 with 9 in the example.)

  2. Show that the Taylor series of 𝑒𝑥 converges to 𝑒𝑥 for all 𝑥>0.
  3. Show that the Taylor series of 𝑒𝑥 converges to 𝑒𝑥 for all 𝑥<0.

Solution.

  1. The remainder at 𝑥=9 is

    𝑅𝑛(9)=𝑒9−(1+9+922!+933!+⋯+9𝑛𝑛!)

    which is bounded by

    |𝑅𝑛(9)|≤𝑀9𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!

    We can choose 𝑀=𝑒9 since that is the maximum value of |𝑓(𝑛+1)(𝑐)| on the interval between 0 and 𝑥=9. Therefore, we have

    lim𝑛→∞|𝑅𝑛(9)|≤lim𝑛→∞𝑒99𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!=0,

    and we can conclude that the Taylor series converges to 𝑒9 at 𝑥=9.

  2. For 𝑥>0, the remainder at 𝑥 is

    𝑅𝑛(𝑥)=𝑒𝑥−(1+𝑥+𝑥22!+𝑥33!+⋯+𝑥𝑛𝑛!)

    which is bounded by

    |𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤𝑀𝑥𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!.

    We can choose 𝑀=𝑒𝑥 since that is the maximum value of |𝑓(𝑛+1)(𝑐)| on the interval between 0 and 𝑥. Therefore, we have

    lim𝑛→∞|𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤lim𝑛→∞𝑒𝑥𝑥𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!=0,

    and we can conclude that the Taylor series converges to 𝑒𝑥 for all 𝑥>0.

  3. For 𝑥<0, the remainder at 𝑥 is

    𝑅𝑛(𝑥)=𝑒𝑥−(1+𝑥+𝑥22!+𝑥33!+⋯+𝑥𝑛𝑛!)

    which is bounded by

    |𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤𝑀|𝑥|𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!.

    We can choose 𝑀=𝑒0=1 since that is the maximum value of |𝑓(𝑛+1)(𝑐)| on the interval between 𝑥 and 0 (note that 𝑥 is negative). Therefore, we have

    lim𝑛→∞|𝑅𝑛(𝑥)|≤lim𝑛→∞|𝑥|𝑛+1(𝑛+1)!=0,

    and we can conclude that the Taylor series converges to 𝑒𝑥 for all 𝑥<0.

§II Warm-Up

(Warm-Up) A traveler wants to get from Town 1 to Town 2 as shown in the figure below, but they must stop by a river along the way. What is the the shortest path they can take? (The optimal path is one of the four options A, B, C, or D.)

Solution. Imagine that there is a mirror image of Town 1 on the other side of the river. Each path from the mirror image to Town 2 corresponds to a path equal in length from Town 1 to Town 2 that passes through the river. Therefore, the shortest path should correspond to the straight line from the mirror image to Town 2. In particular, it is the path such that the angle of incidence at the river equals the angle of reflection, which is option B.

I think this is a extremely clever solution! It is so clever that I don’t think I could come up with it and I find the idea hard to extrapolate to other problems. Luckily, mathematicians have developed the so-called Lagrange multipliers to solve this kind of optimization problems under constraints, and the perk is that it reveals just as much geometric insight and can be applied to a much wider range of problems.

I can give you a glimpse at the idea. Imagine that the river is actually a rail, and on the rail, there is a bead that can slide freely. We can thread a string through Town 1 (as an abstract fixed point), the bead, and Town 2. The shortest path will be revealed if we just pull the string tight.

At that equilibrium point, the tension forces, represented by the red vectors, must be perpendicular to the rail. Hence, we arrive at the same geometric insight that the shortest path must satisfy that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

Lagrange multipliers method, in a nutshell, is this idea that something is optimized under constraint when the best direction of optimization is perpendicular to the constraint. To formulate this idea mathematically is why we begin by studying vectors.

§III Vectors

In a 2-dimensional space, a vector is two numbers put in angle brackets such as 𝑣⃗=⟨8,6⟩. A vector represents only a direction and a magnitude. When we draw a vector, we have to choose a base point, but different choices of base points represent the same vector. For example, the red vectors in the figure below both represent the same vector 𝑣⃗=⟨8,6⟩.

We can also write a vector in terms of the standard unit vectors 𝑖̂ and 𝑗̂ which are vectors with length 1 pointing in the cardinal directions, so the vector 𝑣⃗=⟨8,6⟩ can also be written as 8𝑖̂+6𝑗̂.

There are operations that we can do with vectors. In this discussion, we will just talk about addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication.

  • To add vectors, we put them head to tail. Algebraically, we simply add the corresponding components, so for example, ⟨1,2⟩+⟨3,4⟩=⟨4,6⟩.

  • There are two ways to think about 𝑎⃗−𝑏⃗. One way is to think about it as the vector that when we add it to 𝑏⃗, we get 𝑎⃗. Another way is to think of it as adding −𝑏⃗ (the vector with the same length as 𝑏⃗ but in the opposite direction) to 𝑎⃗. The latter has the benefit of obtaining a vector at the same base point.

    Algebraically, we can calculate it by subtracting the corresponding components, so for example, ⟨1,3⟩−⟨3,4⟩=⟨−2,−1⟩.

  • To scale a vector by a number, we multiply the length of the vector by that number (direction will be flipped if negative). Algebraically, we can calculate it by multiplying each component by that number, so for example, 2⟨1,3⟩=⟨2,6⟩.
