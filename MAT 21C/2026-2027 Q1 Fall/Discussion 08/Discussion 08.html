§II Partial Derivatives

A two-variable function 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦) has two partial derivatives, 𝑓𝑥(𝑥,𝑦) and 𝑓𝑦(𝑥,𝑦). They represent how fast 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦) changes as we take a small step in the 𝑥-direction and 𝑦-direction, respectively.

We have already seen this in discussion 6, two weeks ago! We were doing the exercise of determining whether a function 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦) will increase or decrease as we move in the positive 𝑥-direction and in the positive 𝑦-direction starting at various points.

At point 𝐴, if we take a small step in the 𝑥-direction (towards the right), we will go up the hill and the rate at which we are going up is exactly the slope of the function in Figure 3 at the point 𝐴. Thus, we can say that 𝑓𝑥>0 at the point 𝐴. Similarly, we can also see that 𝑓𝑦>0 at the point 𝐴 because we are also going up the hill at some steady rate if we take a small step in the 𝑦-direction.

Computationally, to find the derivative of 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦) with respect to 𝑥, we treat 𝑦 as a constant and vice versa. For example, if 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦)=𝑥4𝑦3, then 𝑓𝑥=4𝑥3𝑦3 and 𝑓𝑦=3𝑥4𝑦2.

  • The 𝑥-derivative of 𝑥413 is 4𝑥313.
  • The 𝑥-derivative of 𝑥423 is 4𝑥323.
  • The 𝑥-derivative of 𝑥4𝜋3 is 4𝑥3𝜋3.
  • The 𝑥-derivative of 𝑥4𝑦3 is 4𝑥3𝑦3.

When taking the 𝑥-derivative, we treat 𝑦 as a constant just like 1, 2, and 𝜋 are constants.

Another notation for partial derivative is that

𝑓𝑥(𝑥,𝑦)=𝜕𝑓𝜕𝑥(𝑥,𝑦)and𝑓𝑦(𝑥,𝑦)=𝜕𝑓𝜕𝑦(𝑥,𝑦).

§III Chain Rule

Let 𝑧=𝑓(𝑥,𝑦) be a function of 𝑥 and 𝑦, we have discussed that

𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥and𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦

represent how fast 𝑧 changes as we take a small step in the 𝑥-direction and 𝑦-direction, respectively. Now, if we take a small step 𝑑𝑥 in the 𝑥-direction and a small step 𝑑𝑦 in the 𝑦-direction, the change in 𝑧 is going to be the sum of

𝑑𝑧=𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥𝑑𝑥+𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦𝑑𝑦.

The same idea leads to the multivariate chain rule

𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥𝑑𝑥𝑑𝑡+𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦𝑑𝑦𝑑𝑡

when 𝑥 and 𝑦 are functions of 𝑡. For example, if we have 𝑧=𝑥𝑦, 𝑥=cos𝑡, and 𝑦=sin𝑡, then

𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥=𝑦=sin𝑡,𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦=𝑥=cos𝑡,𝑑𝑥𝑑𝑡=−sin𝑡,𝑑𝑦𝑑𝑡=cos𝑡.

Putting these together, we have

𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥𝑑𝑥𝑑𝑡+𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦𝑑𝑦𝑑𝑡=𝑦(−sin𝑡)+𝑥(cos𝑡)=cos2𝑡−sin2𝑡.

This is exactly the same result when we were doing the exercise about composing curve 𝑟⃗=(cos𝑡,sin𝑡) with the function 𝑓(𝑥,𝑦)=𝑥𝑦 in discussion 7. We first substituted

𝑧=𝑓(𝑟⃗)=𝑓(cos𝑡,sin𝑡)=cos𝑡sin𝑡

and then computed the derivative

𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=cos2𝑡−sin2𝑡.

Chain rule allows us to swap the order and do derivative and then substitution. Both ways are valid and have their own advantages.

A more general version of chain rule applies to partial derivative as well. Given 𝑧(𝑥,𝑦), 𝑥(𝑢,𝑣), and 𝑦(𝑢,𝑣), we have

𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑢=𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥𝜕𝑥𝜕𝑢+𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦𝜕𝑦𝜕𝑢.

(Exercise) Find the following, determine whether the chain rule is easier or the substitution-first method is easier.

  1. Let 𝑧=ln(2𝑥+3𝑦), 𝑥=𝑢+3𝑣, and 𝑦=𝑢−2𝑣, find 𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑢 and 𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑣.
  2. Let 𝑧=𝑥4𝑦3, 𝑥=1+𝑡5, and 𝑦=1+2𝑡5, find 𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡 at 𝑡=0.

Solution.

  1. This problem can be easily done with substitution, we have

    𝑧=ln(2(𝑢+3𝑣)+3(𝑢−2𝑣))=ln(5𝑢).

    So we have

    𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑢=55𝑢=1𝑢and𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑣=0.

    We can definitely still do it with the chain rule as well. We have found in the previous exercise that

    𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥=22𝑥+3𝑦and𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦=32𝑥+3𝑦.

    We also have

    𝜕𝑥𝜕𝑢=1and𝜕𝑦𝜕𝑢=1

    and so

    𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑢=22𝑥+3𝑦⋅1+32𝑥+3𝑦⋅1=52𝑥+3𝑦=1𝑢.

    Similarly, we have

    𝜕𝑥𝜕𝑣=3and𝜕𝑦𝜕𝑣=−2

    and so

    𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑣=22𝑥+3𝑦⋅3+32𝑥+3𝑦⋅(−2)=0.

    Sure enough, we get the same answers using both methods.

  2. Chain rule shines in this problem. We computed in the previous part that

    𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑥=4𝑥3𝑦3and𝜕𝑧𝜕𝑦=3𝑥4𝑦2,

    We also have

    𝜕𝑥𝜕𝑡=15and𝜕𝑦𝜕𝑡=25.

    So we have

    𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=4𝑥3𝑦3⋅15+3𝑥4𝑦2⋅25.

    Instead of expanding 𝑥 and 𝑦 into expressions in terms of 𝑡 and plugging in 𝑡=0, it is simpler to the values of 𝑥 and 𝑦 at 𝑡=0 and then plug them into the expression above. At 𝑡=0, we have

    𝑥=1and𝑦=1,

    so

    𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=415+325=105.

    We can write 𝑑𝑧/𝑑𝑡 completely in terms of 𝑡 first then plug in 𝑡=0. Doing this, we have

    𝑑𝑧𝑑𝑡=4(1+𝑡5)3(1+2𝑡5)3⋅15+3(1+𝑡5)4(1+2𝑡5)2⋅25=415+325

    which is of course the same answer but has a lot to write. Knowing to compute 𝑥 and 𝑦 at 𝑡=0 and plugging them in is a very useful trick.
